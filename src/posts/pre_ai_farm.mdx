Some days I catch myself wondering what it was like to code before AI. And some days I wonder what it would be like to not code at all. This post is both of those thoughts — a look back at the pre-AI workflow, and a half-serious plan for the day I trade the terminal for a tractor.

## Tech Before AI

Before Copilot, before ChatGPT, before autocomplete knew my codebase — this is how the work actually got done.

### 1. Debugging Was a Hunt

No AI to read the stack trace for you. You read it yourself, line by line, then:

- `console.log` / `print` everywhere — the poor man's debugger
- `git bisect` to find the commit that broke everything
- Re-reading your own code from yesterday, wondering who wrote it

```bash
# The pre-AI debugging ritual
git bisect start
git bisect bad HEAD
git bisect good <last-known-good-commit>
```

### 2. Stack Overflow Was the Oracle

Every error message went straight into Google, prefixed with the language name. You'd scroll past five wrong answers to find the one with a green checkmark from 2012 that still worked.

### 3. Docs Were Read, Not Asked

You opened the official docs and *read* them. All of them. The API reference, the changelog, the migration guide. Knowledge was built by reading, not by asking.

### 4. Context Was Everything

The hardest part wasn't writing code — it was *holding the whole system in your head*. Every file, every dependency, every edge case. Senior engineers were paid for their context, not their typing speed.

### 5. Slower, but Deeper

Without AI, you couldn't skip the boring parts. You learned the framework internals, the build tool, the database. It took longer, but the understanding stuck. There's a reason old-timers say *"you don't really know a technology until you've debugged it without AI."*

## What I'd Do If I Switched to the Farm

Now the other half. If I ever quit tech and moved to a farm — not as a metaphor, but as a plan — here's what that would look like.

### The Setup

- **A small plot of land** — enough for vegetables, some fruit trees, and a few chickens
- **A simple house** — solar panels, a well, and a wood stove
- **No SaaS subscriptions** — the only recurring cost is seeds and feed

### The Daily Routine

- **05:30** — Feed the chickens, water the garden
- **07:00** — Breakfast, plan the day
- **08:00** — Work the fields: planting, weeding, harvesting
- **12:00** — Lunch, rest during the heat
- **15:00** — Maintenance: fences, tools, irrigation
- **18:00** — Dinner, read, sleep early

### Why It's Tempting

- **Tangible results** — you plant a seed, you get a tomato. No code review required.
- **No on-call** — the farm doesn't page you at 3 AM because a server is down.
- **Physical work** — your body gets used, not just your brain.
- **Simple feedback loop** — the soil tells you immediately if you did something wrong.

### The Honest Reality

Farming is hard. It's early mornings, bad weather, and unpredictable markets. The romantic version — *"just grow your own food and live free"* — ignores that farming is a business with thin margins and no weekends.

But that's exactly why it's a good *thought experiment*. It forces you to ask: *what am I actually optimizing for?* If the answer is *"a life with tangible results and no on-call,"* you don't need a farm — you need a hobby, a side project, or a job with better boundaries.

## The Takeaway

Pre-AI tech was slower and deeper. Post-AI tech is faster and shallower. And the farm? The farm is the reminder that the best tool is the one that gives you a life you actually want to live.

Whether that's a terminal, a tractor, or both — the point is to choose deliberately.
